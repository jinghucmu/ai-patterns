Method

Every pattern here comes from a product I designed. I write down what users needed, the problem, what we did, where it was hard, how it fails and what it led to, and I state each pattern so that a team in any field can use it.

Where the patterns come from

I do not collect patterns from other people's products on this site. Each one is drawn from work I led or contributed to as a product designer, and each pattern page names the product it came from under "In my work". My reviews of other companies' AI products live on a separate site, AI product design reviews.

Because the source is my own work, I can say things a survey of products cannot: what the real difficulty was, which part of the problem turned out to be easy, and how the design behaves when the AI gets it wrong.

Stated for any field

The products I draw on are consumer products, and the examples under "In my work" say so. The patterns themselves are written one level up, in terms of a person, a question, a body of content and a decision, because the problems they solve are the same wherever AI meets people who are not technical: a recording to get through, an answer to check, a mistake to catch. A team building for patients, for shippers or for readers should be able to take a pattern from here without translating it.

What a pattern page contains

Every pattern follows the same outline, so that two patterns can be compared:

  • What users needed. What research with the people using the product showed: what they were trying to do and where the product, as it stood, let them down.
  • The problem. The situation a person is in, in plain words.
  • The pattern. What the design does about it.
  • What makes it hard. The tension that makes this a design problem and not an engineering one.
  • When it fails. What the person experiences when the AI is wrong, and what the design does about that.
  • The accessibility dividend. What the same structure gives to people who use the product without sound, without pictures or without a mouse. Not every pattern has one.
  • In my work. The product the pattern came from, where it shipped, and what it led to, at the level I can share.

Each page also shows which stage of the flow it serves: ask, answer, check or decide. The flow itself is a pattern, Ask, answer, check, decide.

From a user need to a result

The order of the sections is the order of the work. I start with the people who will use the product, not with the model, and find out what they need and where they are stuck. The design answers that, and the measure of it is not whether the feature is usable but whether people take it up, come back to it, and act through it. The work at Amazon that these patterns come from reached an audience of hundreds of millions of customers and, as Xinhua Net reported in its feature on my work, contributed tens of millions of dollars in business impact. I give results at the level that is already public.

The themes

Patterns are grouped into two themes: AI across modes, for answers that cross text, images, audio, clips and buttons; and AI for accessibility, for the structure that lets the same product work without sound, without pictures or without a mouse. Most patterns sit in both. More themes will be added as I write up more work.

Pictures

Where a pattern has screenshots, they are of the product as it shipped, and every image has a text description. Patterns without screenshots are not less real; I add pictures when I have ones I can share.
