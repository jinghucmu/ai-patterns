Tap what you see

Put the action inside the frame, at the second the thing appears, so a video turns from display into something you can touch.

Where it sits in the flow

  1. AskThe person has a question, or a thing they want to find.
  2. AnswerThe AI returns something: a clip, a marker, a segment, a generated answer.
  3. CheckThe person looks at the evidence and decides whether to believe it.
  4. DecideThe person acts: buys, moves on, or corrects the AI.

The problem

A shopper is watching a review. The creator brings out a plate of chicken wings, and the shopper notices the air fryer behind it. That is the moment they want it. But a video is a display surface: to get from the frame to the product, they have to leave the video, remember what they saw, and go searching for it. Most of the time they do not.

The pattern

When AI recognizes that something on screen is a purchasable product, mark it inside the frame itself, at the moment it appears. One tap on the marker opens the product page or adds the item to the cart.

The frame stops being a picture of things and becomes a shelf. What the eye sees can be touched on the spot.

What makes it hard

Two things, and neither is the recognition.

Trust. The marker has to be accurate, but it cannot be loud. Visually it must not read as a paid placement, and it must not pop up at a cut and break the rhythm of watching. A marker that feels like an ad costs more trust than it earns.

Timing. Markers have to appear and disappear in step with where the eye already is. The shopper should never be pulled in two directions in the same second: one toward what the creator is doing, the other toward a button that just arrived.

When it fails

If the marker is on the wrong product, the shopper learns to ignore every later marker. If it arrives a beat early or late, it competes with the content instead of extending it. Both failures are quiet; nobody complains, they just stop tapping.

The accessibility dividend

A marker is also a fact about the video that does not depend on the picture: at this second, this product is on screen. Once that fact exists, it can be read aloud, listed, or reached with a keyboard. See Structure is accessibility.

In my work

Video Hotspots are the buttons that grow inside shopping videos on Amazon. I designed how they look, when they appear and how they get out of the way.

Related patterns
